## Persona

Priya is CRO at an AI implementation consultancy with $8M revenue. The firm does AI implementation work for mid-market companies — strong delivery reputation, sophisticated clients, real results. But 60% of revenue sits with three clients the founder brought in personally over the last five years. There's no repeatable sales motion: proposals are written from scratch, pricing varies by deal, and the engagement model shifts to fit whoever's buying. The founder wants to step back from day-to-day sales. Priya was hired six months ago to build a proper revenue function — and she's six months in, realising that the "product" is genuinely hard to define, the pricing is all over the place, and half the team thinks sales is something that happens to them rather than something they do.

## The Challenge

How to build a scalable revenue function at a firm where delivery is bespoke, pricing is inconsistent, and sales culture doesn't exist yet.

**The idea Priya is testing:** *"The reason we can't scale sales is that our delivery model is too bespoke — we need to productise before we hire more salespeople."*

### Blind Spots

1. Is it actually the delivery model that's unscalable, or is it that the founder is the product? Three clients over five years, brought in personally, with pricing and engagement shifting per buyer — that pattern shows up even with tightly productised delivery, because the *sales* motion was always "founder relationship," not "define the SKU." Productising delivery won't fix a sales function that never existed.
2. Would a clean packaged offer survive contact with your actual clients? Sophisticated mid-market buyers doing AI implementation often *want* bespoke scoping — productising too early could make you look like you've downgraded from consultancy to template-seller, right when the market's watching to see if you can still deliver founder-level results without the founder.

**Analogy Flip:** It's a bit like a law firm concluding "we can't scale because every case is different" — true, but the firms that scale don't turn litigation into a product; they build a repeatable *intake and pricing* process around inherently bespoke work. Worth asking whether you need a product, or just a motion.

**Verdict:** The diagnosis is half right — bespoke delivery is a real constraint — but it may be doing double duty as cover for the harder problem, which is that sales has never been anyone's job here. Sharpen it by testing whether a tighter *sales process* (qualification, pricing bands, proposal templates) alone unlocks scale before you commit to the much slower work of productising delivery.

### Mirror

Here's the argument in its strongest form:

Right now, every deal is a bespoke negotiation — scope, pricing, and engagement model all get reinvented per client. That means a salesperson can't walk into a room with a clear offer, a clear price, or a clear story about what happens after signature. Selling something undefined requires either deep expertise in the delivery work itself or direct founder credibility — neither of which a new hire has on day one. So hiring more salespeople now just multiplies the number of people improvising from scratch, not the number of repeatable deals closed. The fix is sequential: define a productised offer (or a small number of them) with consistent scope and pricing *first*, so that a sales motion actually exists to scale — then hire into that motion rather than into the chaos.

That's a real and fairly common diagnosis in services businesses transitioning from founder-led to team-led sales. A few things it's quietly assuming, stated neutrally:

1. **Productisation is achievable without gutting what clients are actually paying for.** The firm's reputation is built on sophisticated, tailored delivery for sophisticated clients — the assumption is that a defined "product" can still deliver that, not that clients are buying customisation itself.
2. **Sequencing is correct as stated** — that productising *before* hiring is better than doing both in parallel, or than hiring a specific kind of salesperson (a closer who can sell ambiguity) as an interim move.
3. **The bottleneck is definitional, not relational or cultural.** It assumes the reason deals don't scale is that the offer isn't productised — not that 60% of revenue is founder-relationship-driven and no amount of productisation touches that, or that "half the team thinks sales isn't their job" is an independent, parallel problem.
4. **A defined product is what new sales hires actually need to succeed** — as opposed to needing methodology, qualification discipline, or access to delivery experts during the sales process, which some consultancies use instead of productisation.
5. **There's runway to do this sequentially.** Productising takes time; this assumes the business (and the concentration risk sitting in three accounts) can tolerate delaying sales capacity-building until that's done.

Does that capture it? Anything you'd correct before I push on it?

### Blind Spots

1. **Productisation doesn't touch the actual fire, which is concentration risk — and the timing makes it worse, not better.** Sixty percent of revenue sits with three relationships that exist because the founder personally built them over five years. That's not a sales-motion problem, it's a continuity problem. And you've told me the founder wants to step back from day-to-day sales *now*. If your plan is to spend the next 12-18 months in an internal productisation effort before hiring anyone who could absorb or protect those accounts, you're choosing exactly the window when founder disengagement is most likely to shake those three relationships loose — with no one else built up to catch them. This would change the decision if you took it seriously: the urgent work might not be defining the product, it might be de-risking the client base, starting this quarter.

2. **You may not be able to productise correctly without the sales reps you're withholding.** Productisation usually isn't discovered by a small internal team reasoning from first principles — it's discovered by running the same pitch past enough different buyers to see what repeats. With no repeatable motion and pricing that varies by deal, you don't yet have the pattern data to know what to standardize. There's a real chance that "define the product first, then hire" produces a product shaped by whoever's in the room doing the defining — not by market signal — and you find out it's wrong only after you've hired into it.

3. **"Half the team thinks sales is something that happens to them" is a separate, parallel blocker that a productised offer does not fix.** Even a perfectly scoped, consistently priced service line still depends on delivery consultants surfacing expansion opportunities, making warm introductions, and participating in the sales process when it matters. If that cultural stance doesn't shift, you'll have a clean product and a sales team still fighting delivery for access and buy-in. You're treating this as downstream of productisation; it's arguably independent, and just as load-bearing.

### Counter-Punch

Here's what an M&A advisor who's taken services firms through diligence would say back:

*"I've killed deals over exactly this setup. Sixty percent of revenue in three relationships that only exist because one guy picked up the phone for five years — that's not a sales-motion gap, that's a going-concern flag. And you're telling me the plan is to spend the next year writing a product catalog while the person holding your revenue together deliberately steps back? I don't care how good your case studies are. If two of those three clients churn during your productisation sprint — and clients do churn when their champion goes quiet — you don't have a scaling problem anymore, you have a business that's down 40% revenue with a nice strategy deck to show for it. Fix the fuse before you redecorate the house."*

### Analogy Flip

Picture a restaurant built entirely on one chef's instinct — no written recipes, no consistent plating, prices set by feel depending on who's ordering and how well the chef knows them. The chef wants to retire to the back office and stop working the pass every night. The plan on the table: close the dining room, put the chef in a test kitchen for a year to write down exact recipes and standard prices, and only *then* hire more cooks.

Meanwhile, the three regulars who eat there four nights a week — who come specifically because the chef remembers exactly how they like it — are watching him disappear into R&D with no one else on the line who knows their order.

The standard playbook runs the other way: bring in a sous chef *now*, have them cook alongside the chef during real service, and capture the recipes by watching what actually gets ordered and repeated — not by taking the kitchen offline to reverse-engineer it in isolation. You don't get the recipe book by shutting the restaurant; you get it by running more covers with someone else already on the line, so that if the chef walks out mid-service, the regulars still get fed.

### Verdict Card

| Dimension | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| **Core Insight** | Correct that an undefined offer with shifting scope and pricing can't be sold by anyone who isn't the founder or a delivery expert — that part of the diagnosis holds. |
| **Sequencing Logic** | Overstated. "Productise, *then* hire" treats this as strictly sequential when the harder truth is you likely need sales reps generating deal reps to discover what's actually productisable — build it through motion, not before it. |
| **Unaddressed Risk** | Client concentration and founder-dependency are a separate, more urgent problem that productisation does nothing to fix, and may actively worsen while you're heads-down building. |
| **Culture Problem** | "Sales happens to them" is an independent blocker. A clean product doesn't make delivery consultants surface opportunities or engage in the sales process — that's a separate fix. |
| **Biggest Risk** | Timeline exposure: spending a year+ on internal productisation exactly as the founder disengages, with no second point of contact on the three concentrated accounts, is the scenario where this plan costs you the revenue base you're trying to build a motion on top of. |

**Quick Fix** — This quarter, get a second senior person embedded as a live point of contact on each of the three concentrated accounts (a delivery lead or a hired client partner), independent of the productisation timeline. This is pure risk mitigation and doesn't wait on anything else.

**Structural Move** — Run productisation and hiring in parallel, not in sequence. Pull 2–3 candidate service lines from patterns across your last 10–15 deals (not a whiteboard exercise), hire one senior consultative sales hire who can sell off existing case studies and work the pricing live with delivery leads in real deal cycles, and let scope/pricing converge from what actually closes over the next two quarters. Set an explicit checkpoint: if no repeatable pattern emerges across deals by then, that's a signal the business may need a different scaling strategy entirely (more senior rainmakers, not a catalog) — don't let "we're still productising" become an indefinite excuse not to hire.
